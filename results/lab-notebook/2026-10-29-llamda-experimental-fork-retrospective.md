# llamda: what the experimental fork established, and what it did not fix

**Written:** October 29, 2026. **Scope:** the V620 integration, expansion into an AMD fork, model and cache experiments, and October 24 performance-patch investigation. This is a retrospective, not a new benchmark. I have retained the [experimental patches and source ledger](../raw-logs/2026-10-29-llamda-thread-archive/README.md) so the implementation history does not depend on the original conversation.

I am removing the llamda announcement. I built an experimental llama.cpp fork, but I did not establish a consistently faster AMD inference engine. Its practical generation performance was poor relative to what I subsequently obtained with Maya, and it did not deliver the execution strategy that made Maya, Strata, and other specialized engines worth investigating. That distinction matters: the notebook contains measured Maya results on Galactus, but no matched Galactus benchmark for Strata or Gufo. I cannot turn an architectural comparison or another developer's result into a measurement I made.

The useful outcome was an investigation of where llama.cpp lost time when routed expert weights exceeded VRAM. Custom kernels and successful model loading did not by themselves solve that problem. The later GLM comparison showed almost identical throughput from upstream and my fork, while placement, expert residency, and the speculative execution path changed the operating result much more substantially.

## The machine and the earlier evidence

Galactus has an EPYC 7713-class processor, 2 TiB of eight-channel DDR4, and four Radeon Pro V620s. Earlier notes sometimes call the processor a 7713P; the engine output identifies a 7713. The GPUs report gfx1030, wave size 32, no VMM, and 30,704 MiB each: 122,816 MiB available in the device reports, rather than an assumption that all nominal 128 GB can hold weights. The July work used 1 TB RAM; the later STREAM and common-baseline records followed the 2 TB upgrade. I keep those populations separate.

The notebook already preserves the [build history](2026-03-23-galactus-build.md), [April thread/batch experiments](2026-04-07-to-17-thread-and-batch-tests.md), [July diagnostics](2026-07-14-diagnostic-reconstruction.md), [August STREAM rebaseline](2026-08-15-stream-rebaseline-2tb.md), and [five-model August baseline](2026-08-15-to-16-common-baseline-2tb.md). I recovered those records from 46 original files, retained the archive, and reordered entries chronologically. File modification times provide labeled date inferences where the captures lack timestamps; they are not proof of the exact execution time.

The clearest controlled result preceding the fork came from GLM-5.2 in July. Prefill measured 104.97 tokens/s initially, 105.71 after distributing work, and 119.36 after also bypassing a blocking routing-index readback. That was a 13.7% combined improvement; decode remained about 5.51 tokens/s. Distribution alone barely moved throughput. This is the evidence for my scheduler patch, not evidence that every later model received the same gain. [July comparison](2026-07-21-prefill-patch.md)

## Building the fork

I began with [Edwin Brown's RDNA2 fork](https://github.com/edwinbrowwn/llama.cpp-rdna2), intending to run DeepSeek-V4.1 with DSpark and GLM-5.3-Flash with MTP. I asked to rebase onto upstream before accumulating more patches, and later replaced the older DeepSeek implementation with Daniel Han and Unsloth's PR 30817 at `b72050d03ae14bb3cb5138ecefab3f3f65157d31`. That replacement changed the implementation under test; the older vcruz305/JigSawPT results cannot validate it.

I created a common `amd-main` branch and architecture branches `rdna-dev` (renamed from `v620-dev`), `halo-dev`, and `vega-dev`, including gfx900 and gfx906 in the intended Vega scope. I wanted useful defaults rather than a collection of architecture-specific launch scripts. An initial broad combination of forks was reverted in favor of individual patches. The retained sources included normalization launch handling from mxxm-t, small UMA readbacks from halo-box, and qualified RDNA3 quantized matrix-vector paths from Edwin Brown. The historical [integration record](2026-10-21-llamda-amd-fork.md) and archived credits identify authors and revisions. Source popularity was not a substitute for checking a patch's scope, arithmetic, and dispatch conditions.

I promoted both parts of my prefill patch to the common branch: layer-keyed assignment across eligible GPU backends with round-robin fallback, and the high-density routing-readback bypass. The October 21 promotion records `9fcd346d4` on amd-main, `45ea2f058` on rdna-dev, `42d49821d` on halo-dev, and `f7394fdac` on vega-dev. The policy starts at 32 tokens; the bypass also requires at least eight routing selections per model expert. It can upload unused experts when routing is skewed. `GGML_MOE_PREFILL=0` supplies a control. Neither operation provides persistent expert caching or improves single-token decode by definition.

I also prepared a clean routing-only branch, `routing-prefill`, with recorded revision `3c7efe418`, for an upstream-style submission. Preparing and pushing a branch is not the same as submitting or merging an upstream PR. The branch and source credits are part of the work even though the larger fork did not establish the expected performance benefit.

The repository name changed through llamda.cpp/llAMDa to llamda, and executable names changed from llama to llamda. Renaming executables did not isolate the shared libraries. That became an important measurement problem when two CLI names later printed the same build ID. I also exposed expert-cache budget sweeps in the benchmark interface and documented the distinction between CLI and benchmark arguments.

## Loading models was a separate problem from making them fast

The first GLM attempt failed with `unknown model architecture: 'glm5next'`. Once the corresponding support was present, the loader passed that point. It printed unused NextN-layer tensor warnings with speculation disabled and spent enough time loading that I initially suspected a hang. It subsequently generated text. Those warnings and a slow load were not themselves evidence of a failed forward pass.

An early raw `/completion` request returned about 10.19 tokens/s for 128 generated tokens. A later capture returned 4.42 tokens/s. The intervening discussion concerned an 8192 MiB expert cache and placement changes, but the second response alone does not establish the effective launch settings. I retain these as conversation observations, not a clean cache A/B. The model continued the wording of the prompt before answering; `/completion` is a continuation interface, and this behavior did not establish broken weights. A templated chat request was the more appropriate instruction-following check.

I specified that only routed experts should go to RAM. Shared experts, attention, and other dense work should not be accidentally swept into the same placement rule. `exps=CPU` in the later benchmark commands identifies that intent. RAM residency still does not tell me where the operation executes: small decode work can stay on the CPU, while larger prompt work can upload weights for GPU execution.

The original DeepSeek target-only test used eleven Q4_K_M shards from vcruz305 and a separately obtained JigSawPT DSpark sidecar. It reached 11.2 generated tokens/s on the short ZFS prompt. The next launch failed while allocating another 2464.20 MiB on ROCm0 for the draft. The `ctx_other` message was explicitly marked as normal during fitting; the fatal event was the allocation failure. No DSpark throughput or acceptance result came from that attempt. [Original record](undated-deepseek41-dspark.md)

I questioned the arbitrary n-max 2 setting, asked about DSpark's block size, and requested the actual production setting rather than a deliberately shortened test. The retained later commands used n-max 5, but no output confirms those runs. I should not describe either two, five, or eight as a universally correct DSpark setting without identifying the implementation and sidecar. The planned four-way experiment crossed speculation off/on with automatic/forced-RAM placement, using an explicit equal four-GPU split. The early logs do not fill all four cells for either model.

Model downloads also required implementation-specific matching. A GGUF architecture name, quantization label, and sidecar filename are separate compatibility questions. The mxxm-t export discussion identified a V4.1 DSpark limitation in that fork; it was not a reason to assume every DSpark GGUF would work everywhere. I later selected the Unsloth V4.1-Flash-1017 target and matching BF16 sidecar. One `hf download` command downloaded only the explicitly named sidecar because the CLI ignored the include pattern when positional filenames were also present. That was a download-selection error, not a missing target export.

The discussion also covered Q8 exports, high-precision sidecars, and quantized embeddings. UD-Q8_K_XL is a mixed-format recipe, not proof that every tensor is Q8; BF16 is the actual sidecar datatype, not an unspecified “Q16” quant. A quantized embedding tensor was not demonstrated to cause the throughput gap. Changing embeddings or the main quant would change the experiment and requires quality and performance evidence. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next with MTP and Qwen3.8-27B downloads were requested, but those requests are not completed benchmarks in this investigation.

The early context-cap and sparse-attention questions concerned incomplete ports, not a permanent 16K limit of the model. By the pinned Unsloth source review, sparse attention was already enabled by default. I therefore cannot explain the later performance gap by claiming sparse attention was entirely absent. Model support, attention correctness, and the efficiency of scheduling the rest of the forward pass are different questions.

## Why 150 GB/s of STREAM did not predict decode

My rough bandwidth estimate divided sustained RAM bandwidth by the active expert-weight bytes per token. That is an optimistic traffic bound. STREAM uses regular memory operations; MoE execution adds routing, scattered accesses, quant decoding and arithmetic, attention, synchronization, and output work. Weight traffic can differ across quantizations and speculative windows. The final result depends on the critical path, not the sum of nominal CPU and GPU capabilities.

A useful approximation is `token time = attention + routing + expert critical path + reduction/output + uncovered overhead`. With serial CPU and GPU work, the expert term tends toward the sum of their times; with useful overlap it can approach the larger time, plus transfers and the final join. Neither formula is a fitted profile of Galactus. I did not measure the percentage of time lost to each component. [Bandwidth analysis](2026-10-21-bandwidth-and-placement.md)

CPU-to-GPU copying is therefore a conditional explanation. A CPU-computed expert does not need its full weights copied to the GPU every token. Moving that computation to the GPU can introduce a large PCIe transfer for every miss. A VRAM cache can make this worthwhile when hits avoid enough traffic; otherwise it can be slower than reading the weights locally on the CPU. Attention state, scratch buffers, and the sidecar also need VRAM. A larger requested cache can reduce useful headroom or fail allocation rather than improve throughput.

Unlimited shell memlock limits did not prove that every process locked its weights. Nor did a failed lock imply that SSD I/O dominated inference. Several residency settings changed together during the Maya investigation. The later archive recovered settings that the initial capture lacked, but there was no clean memlock-only A/B. Galactus has enough RAM for these experiments; I did not establish SSD-resident steady-state execution as the cause of the gap.

## What was missing from the execution path I tested

The comparison concerns the October builds and inspected paths, not a claim that every current llama.cpp backend lacks these capabilities. Upstream was actively adding the same classes of optimization.

**CPU and GPU expert work needed to overlap within a layer.** In the cache path I inspected, cache misses could force weight uploads before the GPU expert operation proceeded. That is different from keeping hot experts in VRAM, computing selected misses on the CPU, executing independent GPU work at the same time, and joining only when the expert outputs are required. My round-robin prefill patch did not implement that hybrid decode path.

The [Maya v1.1.24 source](https://github.com/mw00/project-maya/blob/75798d4280dcc282b2f2224417fe8fa11e26ea89/src/ds4/ds4_model.cu) makes this concrete: its asynchronous MoE path publishes routing through a host-service mailbox, runs shared and resident experts on the device, and waits for the CPU response before combining results. This demonstrates an implementation mechanism; it does not prove that every layer in my run used that path or quantify its contribution to the measured gain.

**Prefill needed a pipeline that consumed weights as they arrived.** Maya's inspected prefill code uses filled/freed events around streaming buffers and executes expert batches as their transfers complete, interleaving resident work. Merely pinning a host buffer or making the copy call asynchronous does not reproduce that dependency structure. The consuming operation must run before all of the layer's transfers have finished, and buffer reuse must wait for the actual reader. At a larger scale, consecutive prompt microbatches can occupy different GPUs. PR 29963 addresses that inter-GPU pipeline; it is not the same mechanism as within-layer streaming.

**Speculative verification needed to reuse work across tokens.** The inspected Maya path groups tokens by expert, and its CPU kernels can reuse decoded weights across multiple activation rows. It also schedules draft work against target work. Increasing draft length in a generic runtime can instead increase CPU work, transfers, graph changes, and rejected verification work. Time per committed token depends on the full window cost divided by committed tokens, not acceptance alone. This accounts for why a technically functional MTP/DSpark port was insufficient to guarantee a speedup.

**Placement needed to reflect the machine and the phase.** Prefill can amortize a weight transfer over many tokens; single-token decode often cannot. An eight-channel EPYC with slow PCIe relative to RAM can favor CPU misses, while a different machine favors uploading them. The CPU/GPU split, cache capacity, and thread count need measurement together. More GPUs, more cache, or more CPU threads are not automatically better. Maya contains CPU-lane tuning, but explicit thread settings can bypass it, and the recorded GLM HIP calibration attempt did not complete; I cannot attribute those GLM results to successful automatic calibration.

**Fusion and architecture-specific kernels mattered only where they executed.** V620 kernels cannot accelerate routed projections that remain on the CPU. RDNA3.5/4 matrix instructions are not V620 instructions. Adding NVFP4 arithmetic support does not give RDNA2 native NVFP4 hardware. Gufo is relevant to Strix Halo's unified-memory execution and grouped/fused GPU operations, not evidence that a four-discrete-GPU V620 system will obtain its published rates. The review of [Gufo at d4eaaad438](https://github.com/gufo-org/gufo/tree/d4eaaad438a498bf9ba541afe3d655f70dd4196d) found overlapping normalization, RoPE/KV-write, and activation-quantization work already in halo-dev; no Gufo runtime was imported.

Maya explicitly credits [Niko1221/Strata](https://github.com/Niko1221/Strata) as the origin of its engine and credits ggml for quantization, CPU dot products, and prompt kernels. That makes Strata part of the implementation lineage and an architectural reference, not an independent benchmark row in this notebook. The specialized engines can choose narrower model and hardware contracts and coordinate the whole execution plan around them. llama.cpp must accommodate more models, backends, graph shapes, and API behavior. That explains some integration difficulty; it does not make the observed performance gap acceptable or imply that useful kernels cannot be upstreamed.

## The experimental implementation and its actual validation

I initially tried two smaller changes rather than importing a complete runtime. Commit `fceebbb3f` grouped up to four MXFP4 activation rows on AVX2 to reuse decoded weights. It preserved the existing accumulation order. Twenty-four direct dot tests required bitwise equality, and 36 targeted MUL_MAT_ID cases covered token counts, broadcast inputs, and odd block counts. The x86 tests ran through Rosetta on macOS; their timing was not an EPYC performance result.

The same commit added two event-protected 8 MiB pinned upload buffers per eligible discrete GPU when the expert cache was enabled. Tests covered delayed copies, reuse, tails, nonzero offsets, destruction, allocation/event failure, pageable fallback, and unsupported devices. Thirty-three allocator tests passed on the local CPU builds. This overlapped host staging with transfers; it did not implement concurrent CPU/GPU expert computation or streaming expert consumption. No HIP speedup was measured.

I then required reuse of existing implementations rather than building another hybrid runtime. The first review found that leloch's `moe-cache-v2-pr` at `e3096b046` compiles stubs for HIP, while GenerelSchwerz's newer source executor at `f3f31a788` still rejects essential HIP storage/host-alias paths. The AMD moe-hotcache candidate at `beda44967` documented retracted performance numbers and unresolved prefill corruption. I did not import these as working AMD solutions. That selection was incomplete: the later search found upstream PR 30966, with CPU cache misses, an approving review, and an independent R9700 performance report. It became the next candidate, not a completed merge.

I imported Roman Marchenko's PR 29963 at `3ca7eed13274e406822fa1ba3e69a883560566bc` into `experimental/moe-execution` as `3061e2b96`. It adds shared host-weight/input copies, graph reservation, layer-local offload, and balanced fitting. I subsequently isolated it directly on amd-main `e3f526dd7` as `experimental/perf-29963`, commit `04587fd2f`, to exclude the provisional CPU and staging work from its comparison. The library/common builds and allocator tests passed locally. No Galactus A/B followed in this thread, and neither experimental branch was pushed at its last recorded step.

I imported the patch before finishing its discussion review. That was a mistake in the selection process. The [PR discussion](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29963) contains encouraging independent four-MI50 results on an earlier revision: +28% prefill at 8K and +155% at 32K, little decode change. It also contains changes requested after a mixed 3090/3060 regression. A [separate fork's A/B record](https://github.com/sourenaraya/llama.cpp/commit/acefa7b) rejected three versions, including the October 17 version, after substantial mixed CUDA/ROCm regressions. The patch was a credible experiment for matching V620s, not a proven default for all AMD systems.

## The handoff after this conversation

The [archived October 24 queue](../raw-logs/2026-10-29-llamda-thread-archive/performance-patch-queue.md) records 18 pinned candidates. The first priorities were PR 29963, CPU misses in 30966, small-cache policy in 30848, V620 Q5_K/Q6_K tuning in 27558, and GLM mask-memory savings in 31009. It also records CPU matmul changes, scheduler copies and prefetch, speculative-output synchronization, adaptive MTP, Vulkan concat, and additional Vega leads. Patches with documented shape regressions or unresolved synchronization concerns remained on hold. This was an initial shortlist, not an exhaustive survey, and the full stack was never tested.

The later [October 28 status review](2026-10-28-maya-priority-and-upstream-status.md) supersedes the queue's upstream-state snapshot. In particular, GLM model support was already upstream, and DeepSeek V4.1 merged after the earlier integration work. Model support and MTP/DSpark execution support must be checked separately. The same review records ongoing CPU-miss, batched-copy, graph-reuse, and residency work. It is wrong to say nobody upstream was addressing these problems, just as it is wrong to add reported PR speedups together and call the result a validated engine.

My unfinished work is explicit: no measured hybrid CPU/GPU decode implementation landed in the experimental fork; no controlled V620 validation of PR 29963 completed; no full four-way DSpark/MTP placement matrix completed; no corrected build/library isolation was established; and no Gufo or Strata benchmark on my hardware was retained here. I also did not establish that a different quantization or embedding precision would close the gap.

I will preserve the fork as an experimental record rather than announce it as an AMD performance solution. The measurements support using Maya for the configurations that worked, while the source review identifies a more useful direction for future llama.cpp work: reduce dependencies and synchronization across the complete forward pass, use CPU and GPU work concurrently where it pays, and evaluate each change independently before combining it with another. The archived code and failed tests remain useful even though the announced outcome did not materialize.
